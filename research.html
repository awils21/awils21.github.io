Research

Research

My work asks where political judgments come from, how citizens arrange and revise their beliefs, and when public preferences become government action.

  • Survey experiments
  • Computational text analysis

  • Causal inference

Socialization and predispositions

The origins of political judgment

Political attitudes do not begin at the moment someone encounters a campaign or policy debate. I study how orientations learned in ordinary social life—such as familiar rules of thumb and ideas about competition—later guide political interpretation and behavior.

Dissertation Political socialization

Aphorisms and Public Opinion

This project treats widely recognized sayings as observable traces of socialized dispositions. It combines a large collection of aphorisms, computational analysis of congressional speech, and experiments to examine how this familiar language affects understanding, persuasion, and impressions of political speakers.

Published Democratic behavior

Hypercompetitiveness and Loser’s Consent

With Marc J. Hetherington, Ryan M. DeTamble, and David C. Barker, I examine whether a drive to win at any cost extends into reactions to electoral defeat. Using panel data, the study connects hypercompetitive orientations to weaker acceptance of legitimate election outcomes.

Working paper Political psychology

When Losing Is Not an Option

This collaborative experiment investigates whether activating everyday competitive thinking changes judgments about the January 6 attack. It also distinguishes win-at-all-costs competition from competition oriented toward improvement and mastery.

Beliefs, arguments, and persuasion

How opinions are organized and changed

Citizens rarely process political claims one at a time. My work in this area considers both the structure connecting many attitudes and the social cues people use when deciding whether an argument deserves attention.

Under review Political persuasion

Breaking from the Party Line

With Connor Choate and Timothy Ryan, I study what happens when citizens make arguments that do not fit the positions usually associated with their party. The project separates reactions to an argument’s content from reactions to the partisan context in which it appears.

Working paper Belief systems

The Structure of Belief Networks in Mass Publics

With Connor Choate, I use American National Election Studies data from 1992 through 2024 to map relationships among political attitudes. The analysis tracks changes in network constraint and centrality and asks whether those patterns differ with political knowledge.

Work in progress Political communication

Developing an Aphoristic Home Style

With Connor Choate, I investigate whether recognizable sayings help members of Congress communicate with constituents and cultivate a public persona—and when that rhetorical strategy may instead undermine the message.

Public opinion and policy

When public preferences govern

Democratic responsiveness is neither automatic nor evenly distributed. These projects examine whose preferences reach policy, the institutions that mediate them, and the consequences when responsiveness reproduces inequality.

Under contract Cambridge Elements

When Responsiveness Kills

With Frank Baumgartner, Ryan Brooks, Connor Choate, and Elizabeth Seagroves, I examine the role of racial resentment in public support for capital punishment and in the adoption of death-penalty policy. The project combines historical survey evidence with state-level estimates of opinion.

Book project Political institutions

Public Opinion and Bureaucratic Control

With Mary Kroeger, I am developing a study of how the public evaluates federal agencies and presidential influence over them, including how those preferences move with party control and political context.

Work in progress Criminal justice

The Deadliest DAs

This project studies variation in prosecutors’ pursuit of death sentences, with particular attention to geographic inconsistency and unequal exposure to the most severe form of punishment.

Approach

Questions first, methods in service.

My projects combine experimental, observational, and computational evidence. I work in R and use survey design, text analysis, psychometric networks, causal mediation, and multilevel modeling to connect difficult concepts to measurable political behavior.
