Teaching

Teaching

I teach political science through inquiry and practice. My courses make difficult political questions approachable without lowering the intellectual stakes, and students learn by testing ideas, working with evidence, and evaluating what their conclusions can—and cannot—support.

Teaching philosophy

Three commitments

Curious and rigorous

I encourage students to treat political claims as questions that can be investigated. Curiosity opens the door; careful concepts, evidence, and reasoning give the inquiry discipline.

Active and applied

Students develop stronger judgment when they practice the work of the field. Structured discussion, simulations, research design, and data analysis turn abstract ideas into skills they can use.

Demanding and supportive

Clear expectations belong alongside meaningful guidance. I build in feedback, flexibility, and multiple ways to participate so students with different preparation can make genuine progress toward ambitious goals.

In the classroom

Learning political science by doing it

My assignments place students inside the process of interpreting institutions, building explanations, and evaluating evidence rather than asking them only to recall conclusions reached by others.

POLI 100

Introduction to American Government and Politics

Students connect institutional rules to political outcomes through guided discussion, close examination of foundational and contemporary sources, and activities that model the choices faced by political actors.

POLI 209

Analyzing Public Opinion

Students move through a complete research cycle: posing a testable question, designing and fielding a survey in Qualtrics, analyzing the resulting data in R, and explaining what the evidence permits them to infer.

Mentoring

Research as a shared practice

Since 2025, I have led an undergraduate research lab of twelve students. The lab gives students sustained experience contributing to collaborative projects while building habits of careful coding, data work, documentation, and constructive feedback.

My goal is to make research training both substantive and legible: students should understand not just the task in front of them, but how it contributes to an argument and what standards make the result trustworthy.
