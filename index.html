About

Political behavior beyond politics

I am a PhD candidate in American Politics at UNC–Chapel Hill. My research examines how everyday experiences, personality, and familiar cultural ideas become politically consequential—shaping how people interpret elite messages, evaluate political arguments, and respond to democratic competition.

My work draws on survey experiments, public-opinion data, computational text analysis, and measurement of individual differences.

Research agenda

Three connected lines of inquiry into political judgment and behavior.

Dissertation

Aphoristic Democracy

How the socialization of nonpolitical life lessons shapes political attitudes—and how familiar aphorisms influence the interpretation of elite rhetoric.

Survey experiments · Congressional text · Measurement

Democratic behavior

Competition and loser’s consent

How dispositional competitiveness affects citizens’ willingness to accept electoral defeat and confer legitimacy on opposing political winners.

Public opinion · Political psychology

The structure of political attitudes

How political beliefs constrain one another, how that structure changes over time, and which attitudes organize the broader system.

Teaching

Teaching American politics

At UNC–Chapel Hill, I teach Introduction to American Democracy. My teaching emphasizes clear explanations of political institutions, careful use of evidence, and the connection between political-science research and the questions students encounter as citizens.
